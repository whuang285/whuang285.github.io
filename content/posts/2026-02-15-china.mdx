    <h2>Complete the online arrival card</h2>
    <p>
      One important pre-flight task: foreign visitors can complete China's
      <strong> Arrival Card for Foreigners</strong> online before traveling. This
      is an immigration entry form—not a general customs declaration. The National
      Immigration Administration (NIA) introduced the online service on November
      20, 2025.
    </p>
    <div className="action-panel">
      <div>
        <span className="action-label">OFFICIAL GOVERNMENT FORM</span>
        <h3>China Online Arrival Card</h3>
        <p>
          Complete your entry information through the National Immigration
          Administration's official website. The service is free.
        </p>
      </div>
      <a
        className="action-button"
        href="https://s.nia.gov.cn/ArrivalCardFillingPC/"
        target="_blank"
        rel="noreferrer"
      >
        Open the official form ↗
      </a>
    </div>
    <p>
      After submitting, save the confirmation or QR code somewhere easy to
      access. If you can't complete it in advance, the NIA says you can submit
      the information at the port of entry using a QR code or self-service
      device, or complete a paper arrival card. Some categories of travelers are
      exempt, so check the official instructions if you're unsure.
    </p>
    <p className="small">

      generally uses the same local internet environment; connecting to it
      doesn't automatically make blocked services available.
    </p>
    <p>
      Some international roaming plans and travel eSIMs route data through
      networks outside mainland China. Depending on the provider and plan, that
      may allow access to services that are otherwise restricted on local
      networks. Check the provider's terms rather than assuming every plan works
      the same way.
    </p>
    <p>
      Install apps, sign in, and complete account verification before leaving.
      App stores, websites, and verification processes may be harder to access
      after arrival. Have a backup way to contact family or travel companions

  <section className="section" id="connectivity">
    <p className="eyebrow">03 · STAY CONNECTED</p>
    <h2>eSIM vs. local SIM vs. VPN</h2>
    <p>
      Your connectivity setup affects much more than browsing. It can determine
      whether you can message people, access familiar services, receive
      verification codes, and use travel apps.
    </p>
    <p>
      <strong>
        My preference for most short-term visitors: get a travel eSIM.
      </strong>
      I would not make a VPN my primary plan. Many VPNs are unreliable or simply
      won't connect consistently in China, and a VPN doesn't provide mobile data
      on its own.
    </p>
    <table>
      <thead>
        <tr>
          <th>Option</th>
          <th>Advantages</th>
          <th>Limitations</th>
          <th>Best for</th>
        </tr>
      </thead>
      <tbody>
        <tr>
          <td><strong>Travel eSIM</strong></td>
          <td>Buy and install before departure; no physical SIM swap</td>
          <td>Usually data-only; routing and access depend on provider and plan</td>
          <td>Short visits and a simple setup</td>
        </tr>
        <tr>
          <td><strong>International roaming</strong></td>
          <td>Keeps your regular number; may provide access to familiar services</td>
          <td>Can be expensive without a suitable plan</td>
          <td>Travelers who need their home number for calls or SMS</td>
        </tr>
        <tr>
          <td><strong>Local Chinese SIM</strong></td>
          <td>Provides a Chinese phone number and local mobile data</td>
          <td>Requires a compatible phone and registration; local data is subject
            to mainland network restrictions</td>
          <td>Longer stays or services that need a local number</td>
        </tr>
        <tr>
          <td><strong>VPN</strong></td>
          <td>Can route an existing connection differently</td>
          <td>Many services are unreliable or blocked; legal considerations apply;
            does not provide data</td>
          <td>Only as a researched, supplementary option—not your only plan</td>
        </tr>
      </tbody>
    </table>
    <h3>Travel eSIM: the easiest option for many visitors</h3>
    <p>
      A travel eSIM is often the most convenient choice for a short trip. You
      can purchase it before departure and install it without swapping physical
      SIM cards. Some providers route data outside mainland China, which may
      make certain international apps accessible without a separate VPN. This
      varies by provider and plan.
    </p>
    <p>Before buying, check:</p>
    <ul>
      <li>Whether mainland China is included in the plan's coverage.</li>
      <li>How much high-speed data is included and what happens after the limit.</li>
      <li>Whether hotspot/tethering is supported.</li>
      <li>When the plan activates—on installation or on first connection.</li>
      <li>Whether the provider explains how traffic is routed and which services
        are supported.</li>
      <li>Whether your phone is unlocked and supports eSIM.</li>
    </ul>
    <p>
      If you need SMS verification codes on your regular number, check whether
      your home SIM can remain active alongside the eSIM and understand any
      roaming charges.
    </p>
    <h3>Local Chinese SIM: useful if you need a Chinese number</h3>
    <p>
      A local SIM can be useful if you need a Chinese phone number. Some local
      services and account-verification processes work more smoothly—or may
      require registration—with a mainland number.
    </p>
    <p>
      In practice, standard visitor SIMs from mainland carriers are generally
      physical SIM cards rather than a convenient travel eSIM option. Check
      current carrier availability and your phone's compatibility before
      planning around one. If you decide to get one, I would look for a carrier
      shop in the city rather than making the airport your first stop. Bring
      your passport; real-name registration is required.
    </p>
    <div className="personal-note">
      <strong>My phone situation:</strong> I use a U.S. iPhone model that has no
      physical SIM slot, so a physical mainland SIM isn't an option for me. This
      is worth checking before your trip—U.S. iPhone models from iPhone 14
      onward are eSIM-only, while models sold in other markets may differ. If
      you have a U.S. phone, confirm whether it has a physical SIM tray before
      counting on a local SIM.
    </div>
    <p>
      A Chinese number can be helpful for services that use local SMS
      verification. For example, Meituan (美团), a popular food-delivery and
      local-services app, may be more difficult to use without a mainland
      number. I was able to create an account in the U.S. using my U.S. number
      before leaving, but I haven't confirmed whether that setup will continue
      to work reliably once in China. Treat that as something to test—not a
      guarantee.
    </p>
    <h3>VPN: don't make it your main plan</h3>
    <p>
      A VPN changes how an existing internet connection is routed; it does not
      provide mobile data. VPN availability and performance can change, and
      there are legal considerations. I would not assume that a VPN you
      subscribe to at home will connect reliably once you arrive. If you
      research one, do so before departure, review current official guidance,
      and keep a travel eSIM or roaming plan as your actual connectivity plan.
    </p>

    <h3>The apps I'd prioritize</h3>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Alipay — payments and everyday services.</strong> Set this up
        before you leave. It can also provide access to supported transport
        features, bike rentals, and mini-programs.</li>
      <li><strong>WeChat — messaging and a second payment option.</strong> WeChat
        is widely used for messaging, payments, and mini-programs. It is worth
        having even if you mainly use Alipay to pay.</li>
      <li><strong>Amap (Gaode Maps / 高德地图) — local navigation.</strong> Useful
        for local place searches, walking directions, and routes. Apple Maps is
        another option for many iPhone users.</li>
      <li><strong>DiDi — ride-hailing.</strong> Set it up before you need your
        first ride. It supports overseas visitors using foreign numbers and
        eligible international cards, subject to current registration
        requirements.</li>
      <li><strong>Meituan (美团) — food delivery and local services.</strong> Food
        delivery is fast and convenient in many Chinese cities. If you can get
        the app and account working, it is worth trying. Account and
        phone-number requirements can vary, so test it before your trip if
        possible.</li>
      <li><strong>Railway 12306 or Trip.com — trains.</strong> The official China
        Railway app and website are useful for schedules and tickets. Trip.com
        may be easier for some international visitors; compare fees and ticket
        conditions.</li>
      <li><strong>A translator — language support.</strong> Choose one with
        camera, text, and speech translation. Download offline language
        resources where available.</li>

    <ul>
      <li>Install the official app and update it.</li>

        number.</li>
      <li>Complete identity checks if requested.</li>
      <li>Add a payment method where applicable.</li>
      <li>Check language settings and permissions.</li>
      <li>Test the features you expect to use.</li>
      <li>Save essential information outside the app in case you can't access your
        account.</li>

  <section className="section" id="payments">
    <p className="eyebrow">05 · PAYMENTS</p>
    <h2>Set up both Alipay and WeChat Pay</h2>
    <p>
      Mobile payments are one of the most important things to prepare before
      visiting China. QR-code payments are common at restaurants, shops,
      convenience stores, and many smaller businesses.
    </p>
    <p>
      International visitors can generally link eligible overseas bank cards to
      both Alipay and WeChat Pay. You don't necessarily need a Chinese bank
      account for basic merchant payments, but supported cards, fees, limits,
      and available features can vary.
    </p>
    <div className="recommendation">
      <strong>My recommendation: set up both.</strong>
      <p>
        I would use whichever wallet a merchant accepts, rather than relying on
        just one. Having both ready gives you a backup if a particular merchant
        or transaction doesn't work with your first choice.
      </p>
    </div>
    <h3>Alipay vs. WeChat Pay</h3>
    <table>
      <thead>
        <tr>
          <th></th>
          <th>Alipay</th>
          <th>WeChat Pay</th>
        </tr>
      </thead>

        <tr>
          <td>Main use</td>
          <td>Payments and a range of local services</td>
          <td>Payments integrated into the WeChat ecosystem</td>
        </tr>
        <tr>
          <td>Overseas bank cards</td>
          <td>Eligible international cards supported</td>
          <td>Eligible international cards supported</td>
        </tr>

    <h3>What about the 3% foreign-card fee?</h3>
    <p>
      There is a temporary WeChat Pay promotion worth knowing about. Tencent
      announced on May 27, 2026 that qualifying first-time users who link an
      international bank card can receive a waiver of the 3% processing fee for
      90 consecutive days from their first purchase, on up to RMB 1,000 of
      eligible spending per day. The campaign is announced to run through
      <strong>December 31, 2026</strong>.
    </p>
    <p>
      This is not a blanket removal of the fee for everyone or every
      transaction. The standard foreign-card fee may still apply outside the
      promotion's eligibility and daily allowance. Also, your own card issuer
      may charge foreign-transaction or currency-conversion fees separately.
      Check the fee shown in the app before confirming a payment.
    </p>
    <p>
      Alipay has not announced the same first-time-user promotion in the sources
      I checked. Its standard international-card fee rules may still apply. In
      general, foreign-card payments of RMB 200 or less are fee-free, while
      payments above RMB 200 may incur a 3% service fee; verify the amount
      displayed in the app because terms can change.
    </p>
    <p className="small">
      Sources: <a
        href="https://www.tencent.com/tencent-announces-three-initiatives-to-make-inbound-payments-for-international-visitors-easier-than-ever-during-apec-2026/"
        target="_blank"
        rel="noreferrer"> Tencent's May 27, 2026 announcement</a> and <a
        href="https://english.beijing.gov.cn/travellinginbeijing/essentials/inboundtourismfacilitationmeasures/202603/t20260326_4566766.html"
        target="_blank"
        rel="noreferrer">Beijing's payment guidance</a>. Promotion eligibility and terms can change; rely on the fee displayed
      at checkout.
    </p>
    <h3>How to prepare your payment apps</h3>
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Download the official apps.</strong> Install Alipay and WeChat
        from your device's official app store.</li>
      <li><strong>Register and verify your account.</strong> Follow the current
        registration process. You may be asked to verify your phone number or
        identity.</li>
      <li><strong>Link an eligible international bank card.</strong> Follow the
        in-app steps to add your card.</li>
      <li><strong>Complete any requested verification.</strong> Don't leave
        identity checks until you're trying to pay.</li>
      <li><strong>Review fees and limits.</strong> Check the fee shown before
        confirming a payment.</li>
      <li><strong>Understand the QR-code flows.</strong> Depending on the
        merchant, you may scan the merchant's collection code or show your own
        payment code.</li>
    </ol>
    <p>
      Linking a foreign card doesn't necessarily unlock every feature available
      to someone with a Chinese bank account. Transfers, red packets, wallet
      balances, and some mini-program purchases may have different requirements.
      Keep a physical bank card and some RMB cash as backups.
    </p>
    <p>
      For official visitor guidance, see the

  <section className="section" id="getting-around">
    <p className="eyebrow">06 · GETTING AROUND & EVERYDAY LIFE</p>
    <h2>Maps, transportation, bikes, and food delivery</h2>
    <p>
      Navigation can take some adjustment if you're used to searching for
      everything in Google Maps. Have a map app that works locally and save
      important destinations in Chinese as well as English.
    </p>
    <h3>Maps: Apple Maps and Amap</h3>
    <p>
      <strong>Apple Maps</strong> is a convenient starting point for many iPhone
      users. Try it before departure for place searches and public transit
      directions in the cities you'll visit.
    </p>
    <p>
      <strong>Amap (Gaode Maps / 高德地图)</strong> is a local navigation app
      worth considering for detailed place searches, walking directions, driving
      routes, and transit information. The interface may take some getting used
      to, so try searching for a few destinations before your trip.
    </p>
    <p>
      <strong>Google Maps</strong> can be useful for researching places and
      saving ideas before departure, but don't rely on it as your only
      navigation tool in mainland China.
    </p>
    <h3>Ride-hailing: use DiDi instead of regular taxis</h3>
    <p>
      My recommendation is to use <strong>DiDi</strong> for most point-to-point
      trips. I found it very inexpensive and convenient, and it is easier to see
      the destination and fare in the app before the ride. I would generally
      skip hailing a regular street taxi when DiDi is available.
    </p>
    <p>
      Download the app and check its overseas visitor registration process
      before departure. Add an eligible payment card if possible, save
      destinations in Chinese, check the pickup point, and confirm the vehicle
      details and license plate before getting in. You may also be able to
      access ride-hailing through supported Alipay or WeChat features.
    </p>
    <h3>Shared bikes: look for the blue bikes</h3>
    <p>
      Shared bikes are a convenient way to cover short distances. In my
      experience, the <strong>blue bikes</strong> could be unlocked through
      Alipay without a Chinese phone number. The <strong>yellow bikes</strong> I
      tried required a Chinese number.
    </p>
    <p>
      Bike operators and requirements can vary by city, so treat this as a
      practical tip from my experience rather than a universal rule. Open Alipay
      and look for its bike or transport service, scan the bike's QR code, and
      check the price and deposit/authorization details before unlocking it.
    </p>
    <h3>Food delivery: try Meituan</h3>
    <p>
      Food delivery in China can be incredibly fast and convenient. If you have
      the app and payment setup working, I recommend giving it a try, especially
      when you don't feel like going out or want something delivered to your
      hotel or accommodation. Meituan (美团) is one of the major services.
    </p>
    <p>
      The catch for visitors is account access: some services may require a
      mainland Chinese phone number or additional verification. I managed to
      create a Meituan account in the U.S. with my U.S. number before leaving,
      but I haven't confirmed whether it will continue to work once in China. If
      you want to use it, install it and try registering before your trip; don't
      make it your only food plan.
    </p>
    <h3>Public transportation and intercity trains</h3>
    <p>
      Metro and bus payment methods vary by city. You may be able to use a
      transport QR code in Alipay or WeChat, a local transit app, a single-trip
      ticket, or another supported method. Check the local operator's
      instructions and make sure you've selected the correct city in the app.
    </p>
    <p>
      For intercity trains, use the official China Railway 12306 website or app,
      or compare with Trip.com. Foreign travelers generally need to use the same
      valid identification document for booking and ticket verification.

    <h3>Translation and Chinese addresses</h3>
    <p>
      Download a translator before departure and check its offline features.
      Camera translation is useful for menus and signs; text translation is
      useful for addresses and messages; speech translation can help with simple
      conversations. Google Translate's online features may be inaccessible on
      some mainland connections, so download Chinese language resources in
      advance if you plan to use it.
    </p>
    <p>
      Save the Chinese-language name and address of your accommodation, train
      stations, and major destinations. Keep both the English and Chinese

      similar results.
